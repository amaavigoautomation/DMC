What Multi-DMC Really Means

How local destination operations can connect to central distribution and management.

A group operating in several destinations needs a broader view of its business. At the same time, each local DMC depends on its own supplier relationships, products and operating knowledge.

Multi-DMC is about defining how those local businesses connect: what stays with the destination team, what can be shared and where the group needs common visibility.

A successful model respects local operational differences while making collaboration and distribution more structured.

Keep destination expertise close to the operation

Local teams understand their supplier terms, transport arrangements, seasonal conditions and service delivery requirements.

Those details should remain under appropriate destination ownership. Centralising every operational decision can slow the business and separate decisions from the people with the relevant knowledge.

The group needs a clear definition of local responsibilities, including product maintenance, supplier coordination and booking fulfilment.

Decide what the group should share

A common distribution layer can give approved agents access to products across several destinations. Management may also need a consistent way to review activity and performance.

Before configuration, agree which products and channels belong in that shared model. Define who controls agent relationships, selling rules and access.

Shared access should follow the intended commercial arrangement, with local product and service responsibilities still identifiable.

Use common definitions where comparison matters

Comparing destination performance requires more than bringing reports together. Teams need to understand whether the underlying measures mean the same thing.

For example, booking status, revenue treatment and the stage at which profit is reviewed should be defined clearly before group-level comparisons are relied upon.

Local differences may still be necessary. Documenting those differences helps management interpret results rather than assume that every destination operates identically.

Keep the booking’s ownership clear

A booking sold through a common channel still needs an accountable destination operation. The teams involved should know who confirms services, manages amendments, communicates with suppliers and handles financial follow-up.

Where a journey involves several destinations, the handovers deserve particular attention. Customer-facing coordination and local fulfilment need to work together without obscuring responsibility.

A common gateway should make the source and ownership of each service easier to understand.

Plan integration and financial scope carefully

Multi-DMC groups may use different accounting systems, currencies and local processes. Those requirements should be assessed as part of the implementation scope.

Shared sales visibility does not by itself establish consolidated accounting or resolve intercompany processes. Agree which financial outputs and integrations are required, and which systems remain responsible for them.

This keeps the project aligned with actual business needs rather than an assumed meaning of centralisation.

Build the network in stages

DMC24’s Multi-DMC approach connects destination operations with a wider distribution and management model.

A controlled rollout can begin with a representative destination and agreed common processes. Lessons from that implementation can guide later destinations while allowing for local requirements.

The goal is a network in which local expertise remains effective and the wider group can sell, coordinate and review the business with greater clarity.
